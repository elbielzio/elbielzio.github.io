## Strings / themes

**This week’s readings touch on the issues of representation: who gets represented, who controls that representation, and when should we trust it?**

- Maps and archives make certain places, people, and experiences visible while leaving others out. These gaps can reflect exclusion, limited knowledge, or deliberate concealment.
- Recording bodies, movements, and relationships can enable control, as Eddins shows through advertisements seeking the capture of enslaved people. Those records can also be interpreted to recover histories of resistance.
- Turning maps into data and generating realistic images both require examining how representations are produced. Classifying features in historical maps and inventing visual scenes are different operations, with different evidentiary implications.

A shared question we came across the readings is: What does it mean to “fill a gap”, and should every gap be filled?

## Representation: The UN’s “Correct the Map” Initiative

Very recently, on 4 September 2026, the UN General Assembly adopted a Togo-led resolution promoting equal-area projections such as Equal Earth. Mercator enlarges regions toward the poles, making Africa appear disproportionately small relative to them. Equal Earth preserves relative areas, offering a more accurate comparison of continents’ sizes. The initiative raises questions about how familiar maps shape geographical knowledge and perceptions of global importance. Power, colonialism, and violence are the driven forces of understanding how world maps are established in the geopolitical scene.

### Reflection: mapping and collaboration

Digital mapping has the ability to make neglected places and experiences visible, while countermapping can question the dominant representations. However, this also brings up issues concerning who determines the aim of a project, which knowledge is considered valid, and what is made public. When it comes to collaboration, it is necessary to discuss these decisions with the people involved, since different communities may have different views, priorities, and reasons for keeping some knowledge private.

**What would be the way of working with communities in your own area to produce digital maps or counter-maps which reflect their knowledge, question the current representations, and enable the participants to decide what is shown, what is shared and what remains unmapped?**

### Community Mapping: Definitions and Instances

By using community mapping, archaeological research is able to enter into dialogue with the knowledge of those who live in a particular landscape. It involves combining activities such as walking, having conversations, collecting oral histories, taking photographs, and drawing maps together with digital techniques. The people taking part in this process help to decide which places are important, how they are understood, and what information should be made available.

This method takes into account the fact that landscapes can be understood in various ways: while an earthwork has definite dimensions, it can also be linked to memories, spiritual significance, ordinary activities, and histories of belonging. These interpretations differ between and within communities. Digital archaeology creates a contradiction since GIS and remote sensing break down landscapes into categories which may simplify those relationships. The issue then is to make use of digital techniques while at the same time allowing the communities to determine the categories and interpretations themselves. This is related to McDonough's concern regarding the preservation of context when converting maps into data. "Maps as Data."

Instance: Sacred Landscapes Amazonian Archaeology Project – Pando, Bolivia: This project combines archaeological research with drones and LiDAR to address issues of movement, memory, and current relationships with these areas.

It can take the form of countermapping when it questions dominant representations, for example those which depict Amazonia as an untouched landscape. Moreover, collaboration also involves making decisions as to who controls the resulting information and what should remain private or unmapped.
