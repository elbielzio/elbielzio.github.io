Mapping and representation
 This critical reading summary focuses on the Week 5 readings on maps and countermapping, with the handout Niahm and I presented to the class as the most significant influence. Before discussing anything in depth, we need to ask what mapping is and why representation matters. A map, technically speaking, is a visual representation of all or part of an area, often designed to show spatial relationships between features like roads, cities, and bodies of water. But why do we do that? The answer isn't unique; it lies in human efforts to engage with space and place. It is often a contextual artistic attempt to materialize ontologically embodied perceptions of landscape, carried by senses of belonging, ancestry, and memory. It can be seen as data that tell us about a specific story (McDonough 2024). A map of a community in Amazonia, thought and informed by the community members’ own interests, will differ from a map of the same area created by a 16th-century geographer.
 Mapping, even though connected to human relational landscape perceptions, is still shaped by threads of visibility and power. For instance, the Mercator projection has been widely used as the default model for world maps for over 400 years. Created in the 16th century as a specialized navigation tool for sailors, it was later adopted as a standard world map across different eras (Osley 1969).  And why does that projection remain the ‘official one’ for that much time? There is no final answer, but it likely relates to its heavy distortions. By grossly inflating the size of landmasses closer to the poles (like making Greenland look the same size as Africa, when Africa is actually 14 times larger, Mercator’s projection served as a tool of Western narratives about political, social, and biological power over African and other non-Western societies. Greenland, Russia, and even the USA have larger areas than the considered inferior, “third world” places. The model, however, has faced increasing efforts to replace it with equal-area alternatives, such as the UN's recent Equal Earth project, which aims to preserve relative areas and offer a more accurate comparison of continents’ sizes. The important point here, though, is that maps, and the processes of making them, make some people, places, and experiences visible while leaving others out, based on power and influence that impose one specific ontological perception of landscape.

Democratization of representation: limits

 Power and representation impose who appears and who does not in mapping. However, those gaps sometimes can mean different things. They might reflect exclusion, but also something that wasn’t recorded or knowledge people want to keep private. So, making everything visible isn’t always the goal. An interesting example comes from Eddins’ chapter “Fugitivities and Futures: Black Studies in the Digital Era” (2024) that asks how digital scholarship can contribute to Black freedom when the records and technologies it uses are often connected to systems of oppression. The central archival material of the chapter consists of advertisements seeking the capture of people who had escaped slavery. Enslavers placed these notices in newspapers, describing physical appearance, clothing, skills, scars, relationships, and possible destinations. Rewards encouraged readers to participate in capture. These documents therefore had a practical function: they circulated information that could make a person recognizable and vulnerable. They selected descriptions for that purpose. A scar appeared because it might identify someone; a relative appeared because it might too. After all, that relationship might reveal where to search. Today, those same notices preserve names and details about people whose lives otherwise received little documentation. That combination of historical value and violent purpose creates the problem Eddins wants readers to confront.
 
Putting these records into a database introduces another interpretive layer. Imagine an advertisement assigning someone a racial category, and a researcher transferring that description into a field labeled “race.” The resulting entry can make an enslaver’s classification look like an unquestionable fact about the person. In addition, this recovery has limits. The records Eddins studies may tell us a great deal about a person’s sale, punishment, or death while preserving almost nothing about their existence as human beings. In this regard, that imbalance reflects the priorities of the institutions producing the records. Reading sympathetically cannot automatically restore everything they excluded. We may recognize the possibility of affection or solidarity without knowing exactly how a relationship felt to those involved.

 So, my bigger point in this discussion is about the limits of exposure and representation in mapping and archives. The advertisements Eddins discusses were created to help capture people escaping slavery. They made people visible to control them. Yet reading those records differently can reveal traces of resistance and relationships that helped people escape. Should maps reveal everything here? Or are the aforementioned gaps exactly the materialization of the fine line between the democratizing representation and unethical exposure?

Who has the power of representation?

 Mapping and representation are not just about individual scholars or university projects, but about powerful institutions that shape how people exist in the world and understand life. This is a central issue in Jeremy Hsu’s article, which discusses how Google briefly introduced a tool for creating realistic images into a platform people use to verify reality. Google Earth is valuable partly because its satellite and aerial imagery provides evidence about actual places: where buildings stand, how landscapes have changed, or whether a photograph really shows the location someone claims. Adding generative AI to that environment creates a problem because an image can retain Google Earth's appearance and credibility while showing something that never existed.
 
An important part of the argument concerns the authority attached to the platform. Researchers and journalists already use Google Earth to investigate questionable images. For example, someone might compare the arrangement of roads and buildings in a circulating photograph with satellite imagery to check where it was taken. Google Earth therefore functions as a reference against which other representations can be tested. Eliot Higgins, the founder of Bellingcat, objected to introducing image manipulation into that trusted environment. Although the feature generated modified versions of images, its association with Google Earth could make it harder for viewers to distinguish documentary material from images created with its generative tools.
 Fabricated images can undermine confidence in genuine evidence. Once realistic satellite images are easy to manufacture, someone confronted with an authentic image of destruction or wrongdoing can claim that AI generated it. The damage therefore works in two directions. People may believe something that never happened, while others may dismiss something that did happen. Political disputes, for instance, have been decided worldwide based on the influence of AI-generated images.
 
Community Mapping

 Given the power dynamics and inequalities behind mapping, I propose a quick discussion of how mapping can be approached as a collective effort with communities: community mapping in archaeology.  By using community mapping, archaeological research can enter into dialogue with the knowledge of those who live in a particular landscape. It combines activities such as walking, talking, collecting oral histories, taking photographs, and drawing maps with digital techniques. The people taking part in this process help decide which places matter, how they are understood, and what information should be shared.
 
 This method recognizes that landscapes can be understood in various ways: while an earthwork has definite dimensions, it can also be linked to memories, spiritual significance, everyday activities, and histories of belonging. These interpretations differ between and within communities. Digital archaeology creates a contradiction because GIS and remote sensing break landscapes into categories that may simplify those relationships. The challenge, then, is to use digital techniques while still allowing communities to determine the categories and interpretations themselves. This relates to McDonough's concern about preserving context when converting maps into data. "Maps as Data." (2024).
 
 Community mapping could advance this kind of work through shared interpretations of images, significant routes, place names, and local accounts. It can take the form of countermapping when it questions dominant representations, for example those which depict Amazonia as an untouched landscape. Moreover, collaboration involves decisions about who controls the resulting information and what should remain private or unmapped.
 
 My goal with this critical reading summary was not to propose a solution that could completely resolve the problems related to the mapping process, but rather to spark a discussion that encourages us to reflect on the potential benefits and risks associated with the idea of representation.
