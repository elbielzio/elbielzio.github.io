Blog Post 3:

  Before examining critical making in the digital context, it is essential to define making and its underlying motivations. Making refers to the active process of interpreting and deriving meaning from experiences and relationships. Individuals make for purposes such as enjoyment, necessity, or knowledge. For instance, baking bread may fulfill both practical and pleasurable needs, while preparing instructional materials demonstrates engagement with reality through production and modification. In this context, critical making integrates hands-on construction with critical analysis to examine the reciprocal influences between digital technologies and society.
  
  The proliferation of artificial intelligence (AI) models has significantly transformed perceptions of reality, creative practices, and daily life. AI now influences decisions ranging from political choices to culinary activities and medical diagnoses by providing rapid, direct responses and recommendations. Furthermore, AI has altered individuals’ relationships with physical spaces; the sense of belonging and collective memory associated with places, particularly among younger generations, is shifting as virtual realities become more prominent. AI-driven reconstructions of place memory, generated through algorithms and digital traces, often produce representations of the past that may not correspond to actual events, yet these representations shape perceptions of ancestry and the significance of particular locations.
  
  The book 'Critical Making in the Age of AI' (2025) by Professors Anastasia Salter and Emily Johnson discusses the role of Digital Humanities in examining the relationship between society and AI. The authors advocate for moving beyond simplistic binary positions of rejection or replacement, emphasizing the value of questioning, testing, and critically evaluating AI applications. They propose a critical engagement with AI that explores new forms of meaning and creativity while acknowledging the technology's associated risks and limitations.
  
  According to Salter and Johnson, each 'pattern' of use or tool provides a framework for reflecting on creative processes in the era of AI. For instance, maps enable communities to reinterpret familiar spaces and observe changes over time. When approached critically, with awareness of their potential to convey political or ideological messages and serve as instruments of exploitation, AI-generated maps can deepen understanding of ancestry and historical narratives. Mapmaking thus reveals how places acquire meaning and how certain interpretations become dominant. A location has physical attributes, but it is also experienced through memory, ownership, movement, belonging, and conflict. The same site may represent a childhood home, an ancestral territory, or a development opportunity, depending on perspective. The act of map creation determines which of these relationships are made visible.
  
  Beyond maps, games can also integrate effectively with AI. Salter and Johnson (2025) contend that games can communicate ideas and emotions, enabling students to express frustration and build authentic, personalized interactive environments. For example, using games in the classroom can foster creativity and spatial awareness among students.
  
  The authors further argue that AI can be used critically in educational settings, allowing students to analyze patterns in existing works rather than engage solely in corpus-building and cataloging. AI introduces new possibilities for digital creation and analysis, enabling students to collaborate with intelligent assistants in co-creation. This collaborative approach, utilizing tools such as games, maps, and hyperlinks, requires critical examination and adaptation to minimize potential harmful consequences.
