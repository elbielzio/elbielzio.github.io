Blog Post 1
  I couldn't attend Professor Murrieta-Flores’s lecture/workshop because of a scheduling conflict, but I would like to reflect on her texts for our class. Her work makes me think of data injustice as both exclusion and authority. Some histories remain hard to access because their records haven't been digitized or because existing technologies can't process them adequately.  Making those records available, though, does not settle who can interpret them or determine their future uses. It would be making something available without considering the bigger social context. For me, then, machine learning’s potential lies in expanding access while creating opportunities to question how knowledge is organized and who has the power to shape it.
  The research featured in her article “Unlocking colonial records with Artificial Intelligence. Achieving the automated transcription of large-scale 16th and 17th-century Latin American historical collections” (Murrieta-Flores et al. 2025) offers a concrete example. Murrieta-Flores and her colleagues developed models that classify handwriting styles and transcribe colonial Spanish manuscripts from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. In fact, these methods can “democratize" access to information and consequently help researchers search collections that otherwise require extensive specialist training and time to decipher. It is an important intervention because unequal access to historical evidence shapes the histories we can tell and how we perceive and replicate the past. In this regard, making these collections searchable could support research into Indigenous lives, relationships, and strategies that have received less scholarly attention.
  However, greater accessibility does not automatically correct the inequalities embedded in an archive. Colonial documents were produced within unequal relationships, and the categories used to describe Indigenous peoples often served administrative or religious objectives that certainly were not tied to the interests of the Indigenous peoples themselves. For instance, some 16th-century manuscripts of Portuguese colonization in Brazil portray Guarani Indigenous groups as savages without faith, law, or king who needed ‘protection’ and guidance. This portrait permeated the colonial period and deeply and negatively affected those people’s lives. 
  A machine learning model of accessibility could end up repeating harmful categories such as the one I have just described without questioning the assumptions behind them. It is also possible that transcription errors would make some names or experiences more difficult to find than others. If one blindly follows a model, this could involve adopting a project which gains greater recognition for applying an advanced tool to an existing collection rather than for creating a thoughtfully designed archive for a marginalized community that might be interested in using the model (Murrieta-Flores & Shanmugapriya 2026). This key aspect of Murrieta-Flores' work leads me to ask: are we making marginalized histories more accessible, or are we simply facilitating the spread of colonial descriptions? Overcoming data injustice means that we must look not only at the historical source itself but also at the computational and digital decisions that make it into usable data.
  The article “Computational Approaches to Cultural Heritage in the Global South: Infrastructures, Analytics, and Epistemic Justice” (Murrieta-Flores & Shanmugapriya 2026) helps us examine whose knowledge a technology includes and who might benefit from it or face risks. Data justice, not the opposite, should inform a project’s design from the start, not after development. Communities must have opportunities to shape research questions, contest classifications, and influence decisions about access. Their involvement cannot be limited to supplying material for a system whose purposes have already been decided elsewhere. This is especially true because, in most cases, the material that is “democratized” refers to these people’s historical ancestry, memory, and sense of belonging.
  I can relate this data-justice conflict to my own archaeological work with Indigenous communities in Bolivian Amazonia. Because I work with excavations, ethnography, and digital archaeological methods, digital mapping could document relationships between archaeological places and contemporary landscapes, but database categories may fail to capture how people understand and exist in those places. A location can be connected to memory, movement, or sacred relationships that cannot be adequately represented as a coordinate. If machine learning were incorporated into such work, I would want to ask how Indigenous communities would want to use such a model, how they could guide its categories, and which information should circulate.
  In conclusion, what interests me most is combining computational help with shared responsibility in interpretation. Although machine learning can remove some obstacles to gaining historical knowledge, we should also assess its value in terms of the relationships it fosters. To address data injustice, one must consider whether a project gives communities greater control over how their histories become data and how that data is used.
