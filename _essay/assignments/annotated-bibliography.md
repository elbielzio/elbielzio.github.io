Annotated Bibliography

1.	Chase, Arlen F., Diane Z. Chase, Christopher T. Fisher, Stephen J. Leisz, and John F. Weishampel. 2012. “Geospatial Revolution and Remote Sensing LiDAR in Mesoamerican Archaeology.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 109 (32): 12916–21. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1205198109.
 Chase et al. is an important article that opens the door to discussing remote sensing LiDAR in archaeology. Although it focuses on the Mesoamerican context, it defines LiDAR, explains its uses, and outlines its advantages and disadvantages in archaeology.
 The article states that LiDAR stands for light detection and ranging. Archaeologists and landscape researchers use it to record topographic features and anthropogenic landscape modifications. LiDAR works by sending laser pulses from an aerial platform and measuring the distance to the surface, collecting detailed data about the terrain and its features. It can effectively remove preconceptions about ancient size, scale, and complexity, providing a complete view of the topography and ancient environmental modifications. At the same time, although it is considered a revolution in archaeological studies over the last decade, this technology still has several disadvantages, such as its high cost, limited penetration in dense canopy, and seasonal or weather constraints.
  Because I actively use LiDAR in my PhD research in Amazonia, this article is important to my methodological thinking.

2.	Conolly, James, and Mark Lake. 2006. “First Principles.” In Geographical Information Systems in Archaeology, 11–32. Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511807459.002.
  This chapter provides a useful technical overview of GIS and its potential in archaeological study. According to Conolly and Mark Lake, GIS has limitations when dealing with the complex world of archaeology, especially because it presents reductionist representations of reality. However, they also have potential to help archaeologists, given their tools for creating and integrating different types of digital data.
  Along the way, the authors present many types of GIS tools and basic explanations, such as digital cartography, projections (UTM and datum), and especially the discussion of vector vs. raster. Although it is a technical reading, the wealth of basic information about GIS makes this chapter relevant to my own research, which delves into this digital world of analysis.  

3.	Fisher, Michael T., Dovydas Jurkenas, Amina Jambajantsan, et al. 2022. “Multidisciplinary Digital Methodologies for Documentation and Preservation of Immovable Archaeological Heritage in the Khovd River Valley, Western Mongolia.” F1000Research 11:1250. https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.126740.1.
  Fisher et al’s article debates the correlation between digital archaeology methods and salvage archaeology. By applying digital approaches to analyze the geographical threat of the Erdeneburen Hydroelectric Dam to potential archaeological sites and traditional lifeways in the Khovd River, western Mongolia, the authors propose a collective digital archaeology program that could support “proactive” heritage preservation rather than “reactive” measures.
  According to the article’s reasoning, digital analysis has more advantages than regular archaeological surveys because it can easily provide valuable information on the number and type of features that dam construction will submerge or that will survive in the construction of the hydroelectric dam, using computers, free databases, and an iPad with a LiDAR scanner. It focuses on free databases, like Google Earth, Bing, Esri, HERE WeGo, and Maxar Technologies, combined with Python and ArcGIS Pro, to identify heritage resources at a rate of 25,000 sq. km per year.
  Overall, this text makes an important and interesting combination between digital archaeology methods, anthropological questions, and local knowledge. Concerning my research, it provides an important basis for understanding how digital archaeology can help people trace contingency plans that can avoid the complete erasure of archaeological sites, and consequently the human past. It helps me think about memory, ancestry, and conservation.

4.	Guimarães, Maria. 2025. “The Amazon Revealed.” Pesquisa FAPESP, June. https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/the-amazon-revealed/ (Archaeological Project).
  The Amazon Revealed project is a collective effort by National Geographic and several archaeologists, ethnobotanists, historians, and others to scan geometric earthwork sites across thousands of kilometers in Amazonia, mainly in the Brazilian state of Acre. The project aims to reconstruct the Amazonian landscape using LiDAR scanning and GIS analysis to show how dense and complex the forest network of relationships was in the deep past. Led by Dr. Eduardo Goes Neves, my former advisor in Brazil, this project is a pivotal addition to my thinking about earthworks in Bolivia. It helps me connect my work to similar research while also building a network of contacts with other scholars who share similar interests.

5.	Jiang, Hong. 2003. “Stories Remote Sensing Images Can Tell: Integrating Remote Sensing Analysis with Ethnographic Research in the Study of Cultural Landscapes.” Human Ecology 31 (2): 215–32. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1023980713048.
  This article looks at how remote sensing can be integrated into ethnographic research. Because land cover and land use are connected in archaeology, combining ethnographic testimony with GIS analysis could show how people think about the landscape and how digital methods support or challenge those views.
  Using Uxin Ju in Inner Mongolia, China, as a case study, Jian argues that the local community's knowledge of the landscape, especially that of local pastoralists, is quite advanced. Their diverse views on ecological conditions, plant species, and land degradation are valuable for understanding the landscape's complexity and how it is modified. In this sense, the perception of the landscape is inseparable from the values associated with the land. However, ethnographic data is not everything to Jian, as the GIS data she collected, using terrestrial satellite images of Uxin Ju, showed that cultural change in land use over time has led to a better economic life and more options for Mongolian herders, at the expense of landscape degradation and increased need for human effort.
  Jian’s article is an interesting work that sparks discussion about combining GIS methods and ethnography. For me, it shows that, with serious research questions, archaeologists can use both quantitative and qualitative tools.

6.	Llobera, Marcos. 2012. “Life on a Pixel: Challenges in the Development of Digital Methods within an ‘Interpretive’ Landscape Archaeology Framework.” Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory 19 (4): 495–509. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10816-012-9139-2.
  Llobera argues that many criticisms have focused on how archaeologists have used GIS, especially because of a current absence of, or distance between, methods and theory.
  Most who advocate for phenomenology criticize GIS because it does not fully portray the “physical experience of the archaeological sites,” which, to Llobera, is a weak and unsubstantiated argument, since cultural-cognitive and environmental factors are not necessarily mutually exclusive. Feeling the site physically doesn’t exclude using GIS representations. To address this problem, the author suggests developing a “middle ground” that balances theory and method, one that would draw on the distinct techniques, measures, and strategies archaeologists might mobilize when constructing and exploring possible arguments.  
  For Llobera, GIS can be reworked and reinstalled into a new world of interpretation, even playing a “subversive” role other than the one for which it may have been originally formulated. In other words, it is not static technology that cannot be reshaped to meet archaeological needs. This is important for my research because it allows me to argue that GIS is not the final goal of doing what I do, but another important tool that is shaped and reshaped based on what I am asking.

7.	McDonough, Katherine. 2024. “Maps as Data.” In Computational Humanities, edited by Lauren Tilton, David Mimno, and Jessica Marie Johnson, 99–126. University of Minnesota Press. https://dhdebates.gc.cuny.edu/read/computational-humanities-5c64bbab-d7ca-41be-8f87-f26117a9a20f/section/992f5fc5-9a0d-4e9a-863a-adc1de9a678e.
  McDonough is asking how historians can study large collections of maps while remaining attentive to the decisions, uncertainties, and relationships that those maps contain. The difficulty is that computers need information to be organized into recognizable categories, while historical documents often contain ambiguity. A road on a map, for example, might represent something surveyed, something planned, or something copied from an earlier source. If we simply turn that line into a digital object called “road,” we can lose the distinction. The chapter explores how to make computational analysis useful while keeping these interpretive problems visible.
  This interesting chapter I got to know during our week 5 class will make me reflect more deeply on how I use, produce, and search maps for my PhD research. The chapter is an invitation to make technical choices answerable to historical questions. Before counting a feature, decide what the count could establish. Before drawing a boundary, consider what kind of relationship that boundary expresses. Before trusting a classification, return to the source. Working at a larger scale becomes valuable when it expands what historians can ask while preserving their ability to question the evidence.

8.	Pärssinen, Martti, Risto Kalliola, Alceu Ranzi, et al. 2026. “Over 20,000 Precolonial Earthworks in the Southwest Amazonia.” Nature 657 (8131): 420–25. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10835-7.
  This interesting, speculative article about earthworks in Southwestern Amazonia is pivotal for my research. The authors use LiDAR and GIS analysis to predict the approximate number of precolonial earthworks in the modern frontiers of Brazil, Bolivia, and Peru. They also discuss the geographical distribution and sizes of earthworks, suggesting a vast network of relationships among Indigenous peoples in the past.
  The relevance of this article is clear. They show earthworks that were not mapped before (around 1500) and indicate that there are at least 20,000 in the whole forest, which strengthens my research in Bolivia and allows me to analyze sites that could not be seen under the vegetation beforehand.
9.	Tripcevich, Nicholas, and Steven A. Wernke. 2010. “On-Site Recording of Excavation Data Using Mobile GIS.” Journal of Field Archaeology 35 (4): 380–97. https://doi.org/10.1179/009346910X12707321242511.
  This article discusses the potential of GIS methods for collecting excavation data. Unlike well-established excavation methods, the authors argue that a GIS-based rapid digital recording system will improve the recording procedures for all of the archaeologists who aim to use it. They argue for a mixed methodology that embraces both written records and GIS mapping, since some archaeological sites’ locations might be more or less suitable for computerized approaches – this hybrid system would use some records on paper, while the digital registry is left with the task of accommodating standardized excavation observations (e.g. soil color and texture, or collection inventories) and precise measurements.
  Tripcevich’s work is useful for my research because it introduces several digital methodologies for archaeological excavations. The use of nominal attributes, offset mapping, photomapping, and the use of a total station, together with their advantages and drawbacks, to make the excavation faster and more efficient.

10.	Wernke, Steven A., Lauren E. Kohut, and Abel Traslaviña. 2017. “A GIS of Affordances: Movement and Visibility at a Planned Colonial Town in Highland Peru.” Journal of Archaeological Science 84:22–39. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2017.06.004.
  The first article in this week’s class debates GIS's potential to understand movement and visual experience within a planned colonial town in highland Peru, Mawchu Llacta. According to the authors, this place is an extraordinary example of how archaeological/historical buildings are objects of reinterpretation, narration, and representation that can be understood through different kinds of tools. Based on their GIS results, they argue that the reduccion, a compact “town” built by Spanish colonizers in the 1570s, can be analyzed as both a colonized and a colonizing place that displays a new pattern of practice, sensory experience, and surveillance onto the Inkaic past.
  To produce a generalized simulation of movement through the reduccion, the authors use a GIS-based model that combines spatial network and cumulative viewshed analysis to create a transit density map. This includes an anisotropic walking model to estimate travel costs and per-house population estimates to approximate transit density along network paths.
 Given my interest in landscape movement in archaeology, this article offers useful philosophical and technical insights that can help me think more carefully about my own archaeological context of earthwork sites in Amazonia.
